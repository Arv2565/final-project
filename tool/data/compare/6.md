Kerala has recently codified a **single, modern public-health statute**, while Karnataka still relies on a **patchwork of epidemic-disease and municipal laws** for most public-health and sanitation powers.

| Aspect | Kerala | Karnataka |
|------|------|------|
| Core public-health statute | **Kerala Public Health Act, 2023** – unifies and replaces older public-health laws (Madras Public Health Act 1939, Travancore-Cochin Public Health Act 1955, etc.) into one comprehensive “one-health” code. | No single general public-health code yet; relies on the **Karnataka Epidemic Diseases Act, 2020** for epidemics, and municipal laws (e.g., **Karnataka Municipal Corporations Act, 1976**) for sanitation and public-health functions. |
| Structure of authorities | Creates a clear **State, District and Local Public Health Officer** hierarchy with defined powers to inspect, issue directions, impose fines, and close establishments that pose health hazards. | Public-health powers are split: Health Dept + municipal commissioners/health officers. Municipal Acts give commissioners powers over dangerous diseases, disinfection, closure of infected buildings, etc., but not under a unified public-health code. |
| Scope (beyond epidemics) | Act explicitly covers **communicable & non-communicable diseases, climate-change threats, biomedical waste, food safety, migrant workers, elderly and disabled welfare, safe water, sanitation and toilets**; requires annual public-health action plans and earmarked local-body budgets for NCD prevention. | Epidemic Diseases Act, 2020 focuses mainly on **epidemic control** (notification of epidemic disease, special measures, penalties); broader issues like NCDs, climate, or social determinants are only indirectly addressed via sectoral programmes and are *not* codified in a single public-health statute yet. |
| Epidemic-disease powers | Kerala Public Health Act integrates epidemic-disease powers: local officers can order isolation, closure of establishments, restrictions on gatherings, and water/food-safety measures, with due-process safeguards and penalties. | **Karnataka Epidemic Diseases Act, 2020** consolidates and updates earlier epidemic laws; state can notify epidemics, issue regulations (masking, lockdown-style restrictions), and impose penalties for violations; used extensively during COVID-19. |
| Sanitation & waste management | Kerala Municipality Act, 1994 lists **public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid-waste management** as core municipal functions; combined with the 2023 Public Health Act, local bodies are obliged to ensure safe water, sanitation, and hygienic surroundings as part of statutory public-health planning. | Karnataka Municipal Corporations Act, 1976 assigns corporations duties for **drainage, solid waste, slaughterhouses, water-supply hygiene, disinfection, dangerous-disease control, compulsory vaccination**, etc., but under municipal law rather than a separate, modern public-health code. |
| Right-to-health framing | Kerala’s Public Health Act is framed around a broad, proactive public-health vision (one-health, prevention, social determinants), though it does not itself label health as a justiciable “right to health”. | Karnataka has drafted a **Right to Health and Emergency Medical Service Bill, 2025**, which, if enacted, would impose duties on government to ensure safe food, drinking water, sanitation and emergency medical services, but this is still at bill stage. |

In short, Kerala has a **dedicated, unified public-health and sanitation statute with strong local-officer powers and explicit preventive obligations**, whereas Karnataka still operates mainly through **sector-specific laws** (epidemic-disease act + municipal acts), with a right-to-health bill under discussion rather than in force.
