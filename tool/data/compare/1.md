
| Aspect | Kerala | Karnataka |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Main statute | Kerala Land Reforms Act, 1963 (Act 1 of 1964), especially Chapter III (Sections 81–98A) on ceilings and surrender.| Karnataka Land Reforms Act, 1961 (Karnataka Act 10 of 1962), with major 2015 and 2020 amendments.|
| Basic policy goal | Abolish landlordism, give fixity of tenure to cultivating tenants, and **strictly cap** maximum holdings, with aggressive vesting and redistribution of surplus.| Abolish intermediaries and impose ceilings, but now more focused on liberalising who can buy/hold agricultural land and raising or relaxing ceilings.|
| Ceiling framework | Uses “ceiling area” in standard acres under Section 82; typical family ceiling is about **10 standard acres**, not less than 12 and not more than 15 actual acres, with lower limits for single persons.| Uses “units” of land under Section 63; post‑2020, a person or family up to 5 members can hold **20 units**, and a larger family can go up to **40 units**; certain tenants can hold up to **80 units**.|
| Typical max holding for ordinary family | Around **12–15 acres** for a normal family (10 standard acres ceiling, with limits that ceiling cannot be <12 or >15 acres). | With 1 unit = 1 acre of A‑class irrigated land, an ordinary family can hold up to **20–40 acres** of best‑class land, and more in lower‑class categories; some tenants up to **80 acres** A‑class equivalent.|
| Treatment of tenants | Cultivating tenants get fixity and are enabled to purchase landlord’s interest; ceiling and surrender provisions also apply to lands held by tenants, who cannot hold above ceiling.| Tenants’ rights recognised, but amendments significantly relax who can own land; certain tenants (e.g., in coastal districts) get much higher unit ceilings (up to 80 units).  |
| Exemptions from ceiling | Section 81 lists exempt categories (e.g., specified plantations, certain institutions, government projects) and allows government to grant exemptions subject to conditions and project timelines. | Exemptions exist (e.g., earlier for plantations, research farms, etc.), but policy trend is towards easing restrictions and allowing larger holdings and corporate/institutional ownership, subject to revised ceilings.|
| Post‑ceiling surplus | Land above ceiling must be surrendered (Section 85), vests in the state, and is redistributed to landless/eligible persons; strong bar on holding above ceiling.| Surplus over revised unit‑based ceilings is also not allowed, but because the ceilings are much higher (and income/“agriculturist” restrictions have been repealed), less land is forced into surplus compared to Kerala. |
| Who can buy agricultural land | Historically and presently more redistributive and tenant‑oriented; central issue is not outsider purchase but size and nature of holding and exemptions. | After 2015 and especially 2020 amendments, **any Indian individual, trust, company or institution** can buy agricultural land regardless of non‑agricultural income; Sections 79A, 79B, 79C restricting non‑agriculturists are repealed.|
| Overall practical effect | Very **low ceilings**, strong anti‑landlord stance, and continued focus on preventing any person (including tenants) from holding land above a modest ceiling area; large estates mostly dismantled.| Ceilings exist but are **much higher and now liberalised**, and non‑agriculturists and high‑income persons can legally buy significant extents of agricultural land, subject to the higher unit‑based limits.|
